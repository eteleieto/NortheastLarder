*Taraxacum officinale*

Dandelion is an introduced perennial, but in the Northeast it now feels almost native to the food imagination because it turns up everywhere people walk: lawns, field edges, gravelly lots, and the first open patches of spring ground.[^1] Its young greens are among the earliest edible wild leaves in Maine, long before most garden crops are ready, and their bitterness intensifies as the plant matures.[^1]

The plant grows as a basal rosette with hollow flower stalks rising separately from the leaves, which is one reason it is easy to gather cleanly when young.[^1] The leaves are the obvious food, but they are not the whole story. Unopened buds can be brined or pickled into the thing people call dandelion capers, the open flower heads have been used for wine, and the root has a parallel life as a roasted coffee substitute with a chicory-like edge.[^2]

That versatility is old rather than trendy. By the nineteenth century improved leafy forms were already being selected in France for salad use, while roasted dandelion root was a recognized caffeine-free drink ingredient.[^2] Dandelion's ordinariness is part of its appeal as a larder ingredient, but it is also the reason to be careful: the plant is edible, yet the site matters just as much as the species, so roadsides, sprayed lawns, and contaminated soils are poor places to collect it.[^1]

[^2]: John Vaughan and Catherine Geissler, *The New Oxford Book of Food Plants*, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2009), p. 124 (PDF p. 159).
