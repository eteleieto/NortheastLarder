*Vitis* spp.

Grapes are long-lived perennial vines that fruit on shoots arising from one-year-old canes. That growth pattern is central to how vineyards are trained and pruned.[^1]


Maine’s main constraints are winter injury and a short growing season. Native *Vitis labrusca* types and interspecific hybrids provide more hardiness than European *Vitis vinifera* varieties.[^1]


The strong aroma often called “foxy” is characteristic of many *V. labrusca* grapes, including Concord types. It is a varietal trait, not evidence that the fruit has spoiled.[^1]

That Concord-like musk comes partly from anthranilate esters. Table grapes, wine grapes, and raisin grapes are selected toward different structures as well: wine grapes tend to be smaller and more acidic, table grapes larger and less tart, and raisin grapes thin-skinned, sugary, and loosely clustered so they dry more readily.[^2]

[^1]: University of Maine Cooperative Extension, “[Growing Grapes in Maine](https://extension.umaine.edu/highmoor/resources-by-crop/grape/growing-grapes-in-maine/),” 2013.
[^2]: Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen*, rev. ed. (Scribner, 2004), PDF p. 377.
