*Pinus* spp.; Northeast food use most often points to eastern white pine, *Pinus strobus*

Eastern white pine needles have a resinous, citrus-leaning aroma and a long regional association with needle infusions. The growing tips, or “candles,” are the tree’s soft new stems before their needles spread out.[^1]

### Five needles for eastern white pine

Eastern white pine is native throughout New England. A handy identification clue is that its needles grow five to a bundle—the same number of letters as “white.”[^2]

### The dangerous problem with “needled evergreen”

“Needled evergreen” is not a food identity. Yew (*Taxus*) can be mistaken for other evergreens and contains highly poisonous taxine compounds in its needles and most other tissues. Any gathered conifer ingredient needs species-level identification.[^3]

### Bundles distinguish pine from spruce

Pine and [[Spruce 1|spruce]] share resinous evergreen aromas, but their needles are arranged differently: pine needles come in bundles, while spruce needles attach one at a time.
