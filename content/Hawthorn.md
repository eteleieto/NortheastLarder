*Crataegus* spp.

Hawthorns are thorny members of the rose family with small apple-like fruits called haws. Depending on the species, the fruit can be red, orange, yellow, or nearly black and ranges from dry and mealy to fairly juicy.[^1]


New England has a confusing number of native and introduced hawthorns, and even botanists have disagreed over where one species ends and another begins. Fireberry hawthorn, *Crataegus chrysocarpa*, is one northern species whose range reaches from Newfoundland south into the United States.[^1]


Young leaves, flower buds, and young flowers also have a history as food. The fruits are pomes, putting them in the same structural neighborhood as [[Apple|apple]], although each haw contains several hard pyrenes that make the small fruit more seed than flesh.[^2][^3]

Haws vary dramatically between species. Some are dry and mealy, while others have thick, sweet flesh that works better cooked than eaten out of hand. A New England hawthorn may be red, orange, yellow, or almost black when ripe, so fruit color alone is not a reliable identification. The genus is unusually difficult in this region, with many species and hybrids, which makes a confirmed plant identification important before gathering.[^1][^4]

The food value of hawthorn is concentrated in the flesh around the seeds. In species with worthwhile fruit, that flesh can be cooked into a preserve or pie filling, dried, or strained into a thick sauce; the hard inner pyrenes are discarded. Hawthorn also carries a useful seasonal contrast: the fruits persist into fall and winter, after softer berries have disappeared, while the young spring leaves and flowers offer a different, greener food before the haws arrive.[^3]


The name *Crataegus* comes from a Greek word associated with strength, while “haw” is an old word for the fruit. A hawthorn hedge is therefore not named after the modern fruit haw flakes, though those sweets are made from hawthorn fruit.
