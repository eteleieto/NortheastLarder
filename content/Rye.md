*Secale cereale*

Rye has a darker, earthier flavor than wheat and a gentle bitter edge. It is often associated with bread and brewing, but is also an important Northeast grain in its own right.


Rye is the most winter-hardy small grain grown in Maine and fits well into Northeast rotations.[^1]


Rye can release root exudates that suppress germination and growth of some weed seeds—an allelopathic effect that is useful in rotations but can also affect small-seeded following crops.[^1]

Rye flour owes much of its baking behavior to pentosans rather than a wheat-like gluten network. These carbohydrates absorb far more water than wheat flour and do not harden by retrogradation after cooling, helping rye bread stay soft and moist for unusually long periods.[^2]

[^1]: University of Maine Cooperative Extension, “[Maine Forage Facts: Small Grains Crops](https://extension.umaine.edu/publications/2270e/),” accessed July 17, 2026.
[^2]: Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen*, rev. ed. (Scribner, 2004), PDF p. 485.
