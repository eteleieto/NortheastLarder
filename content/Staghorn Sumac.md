---
title: Staghorn Sumac
tags: [INGREDIENT]
---

*Rhus typhina*

Staghorn sumac is a native Northeast shrub recognizable by its velvet-covered young branches and upright clusters of deep red fruit. The fruit’s tart surface gives it its food interest.[^1]


The red fruit clusters persist into winter and are not the same thing as the white berries of poisonous sumac, a different plant associated with wet habitats.[^1]


Staghorn sumac is among the native Maine shrubs documented by UMaine Extension; identification remains essential when gathering any wild food plant.[^1]

The “staghorn” clue is the dense hair on young branchlets and leaf stalks. New England's botanical flora records the species—also published as *Rhus hirta*—in all six states, especially along roadsides, forest edges, and dry fields.[^2]

The food is the coating on the upright fruit clusters rather than a juicy berry in the usual sense. Botanically, each small fruit is a dry drupe with a firm inner wall around one seed, covered by red hairs that carry the tart flavor.[^3] Gently steeped in cool water, ripe clusters make the bright, lemon-like drink often called sumac-ade or rhus juice. Wabanaki plant records also describe the fruit as a beverage ingredient, placing this familiar roadside shrub within a living Northeast food tradition rather than treating it only as a wild novelty.[^4]
