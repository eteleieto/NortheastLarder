*Arctium lappa* and *Arctium minus*

Burdock is a Eurasian biennial now common in disturbed ground around the Northeast. The long first-year root is the main food part; cultivated great burdock is the vegetable known in Japan as gobo.[^1]


The plant spends its first year as a low rosette and sends up its tall flowering stalk in the second. Besides the root, young leaves and immature flower stalks are edible before they become coarse.[^1]

Like salsify and scorzonera, burdock stores food in a long taproot that becomes increasingly fibrous with size and age. Cut gobo also browns readily because it is rich in phenolic compounds; those same compounds contribute substantial antioxidant activity.[^3]


Burdock burrs travel by catching their tiny hooks on fur and clothing. After examining burrs caught on his dog, Swiss inventor George de Mestral used the same hook-and-loop idea for Velcro.[^2]

The food plant and the weed are the same organism, but cultivation changes the experience. A loose, deep soil lets the first-year root grow straighter and makes it easier to lift; an unselected roadside plant usually produces a more forked, woody root. Great burdock can reach several feet in height in its second year, and its purple flower heads are surrounded by hooked bracts that mature into the familiar burrs.[^1] The burr is not just a visual oddity: it is the plant's seed-dispersal system, carrying achenes on passing animals and clothing.

Burdock's long storage root is mostly interesting for its texture and aroma rather than sweetness. Its crispness comes from a firm, fibrous structure, while cutting exposes phenolic compounds that darken the surface quickly. That browning and the root's earthy, faintly artichoke-like flavor make gobo distinct from a plain starch vegetable. The young leaves and unopened flower stalks are edible too, but their window closes as the plant becomes coarse and prepares to flower.[^1]
