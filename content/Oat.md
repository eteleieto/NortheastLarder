*Avena sativa*

Oats are a mild, creamy grain that fits the cool, wet Northeast unusually well. In Maine they remain a substantial field crop, and recent UMaine variety work points to continued interest in food-grade and hulless oats rather than feed alone.[^1] Historically they were often treated as a secondary cereal beside wheat and barley, but that outsider status says more about old bread preferences than about the grain itself.[^2][^3]

What oats do especially well is hold water. Their texture comes largely from beta-glucans, concentrated near the outer endosperm, which is why cooked oats feel smooth and thick instead of fluffy like rice.[^2] Those same gums are useful here in plant milks, porridges, and grain ferments because they add body without relying on a lot of fat or starch gelatinization alone.

Oats also behave differently in storage from most other cereals. They carry substantially more fat than wheat along with active lipase and lipoxygenase, so untreated groats can go stale or bitter quickly.[^2][^4] The standard heat treatment given to oat groats is partly about stability, but it also builds the toasted aroma many people read as the grain's natural flavor. That means the taste of rolled oats is already a lightly processed one.

Most food oats are still the same groat underneath. Steel-cut oats are chopped groats; rolled oats are steamed and flattened; quick oats are rolled thinner so they hydrate faster.[^2][^3] That shared identity matters in this notebook because the ingredient keeps moving between intact grain, malted grain, sweetened dairy analog, and savory ferment. The form changes quickly, but the grain's soft cereal sweetness and thickening power stay recognizably oat-like through all of them.
