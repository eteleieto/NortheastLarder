*Perilla frutescens*

Shiso is a mint-family leaf native to China and India and established in Japanese food culture by the eighth or ninth century. Its defining fatty, herbaceous, spicy aroma comes from perillaldehyde, though some varieties lack that compound and lean toward dill or lemon instead.[^1] Green and red-purple forms differ in more than color: the purple is carried by anthocyanins, while the leaves can also vary substantially in texture and aroma. New England records treat perilla as an introduced plant of roadsides, waste areas, and gardens in Connecticut and Massachusetts.[^2]

In our handling, green shiso has been sturdier and more spinach-like, while purple shiso has been flimsier and slightly more acidic, somewhat like sorrel.

Shiso no ha refers to fermented red shiso leaf.

Red shiso (akajiso) is used as a coloring agent for umeboshi.

The red color comes from anthocyanin pigments, so it behaves differently from the green leaf even before preservation: the purple form carries both visual intensity and a more pronounced savory-herbal presence.[^3] Perilla is an introduced annual in the Northeast rather than a native woodland herb, but it is well suited to garden production and can reseed where plants are allowed to flower. In food terms, the leaf is unusually valuable because its aroma survives as a recognizable top note in salt, vinegar, and fruit preserves, while its soft tissue also takes on the flavor of a cure.

	- Question - to wash or not?

The question about washing dried shiso is partly a question about texture and partly about handling: rinsing removes surface salt or soil but also adds water that must be removed again before the leaf is stable. That makes the notebook's green-versus-purple comparison useful, since color, aroma, and leaf toughness may change independently during drying.
