*Betula* spp.

Birch syrup is a tree syrup made by concentrating the spring sap of birch trees rather than maples. It belongs in a Northeast larder because the same northern hardwood forests that support maple sugaring also carry yellow, paper, black, and river birch, and Cornell now treats birch as a practical extension-season syrup for northeastern producers.[^1][^2]

The ingredient behaves differently from maple syrup from the very start. Birch sap is much more dilute, generally around 0.5 to 1.0 percent sugar in Cornell's recent New York work, so it takes roughly a hundred parts sap to make one part syrup instead of the far smaller reduction typical for maple.[^2][^3] That low sugar concentration is one reason birch syrup tends to be expensive and strongly cooked rather than delicate.

Its flavor is also its own thing. McGee describes birch syrup as relying on glucose and fructose rather than mostly sucrose, which helps explain both its thinner sap and its darker, more caramel-like finished flavor.[^3] Cornell's maple program notes that the finished syrup can read fruity-tart, balsamic, molasses-like, or even reminiscent of tart cherry and apple butter depending on how it is processed.[^2]

Birch is therefore a late-season companion to maple rather than a substitute for it. Cornell’s Northern New York work places birch tapping near the end of the maple run, when the trees are beginning their own spring flow, and emphasizes that the sap must be concentrated efficiently because its low sugar content makes a very large reduction.[^1][^2] The syrup is usually treated as a cooking ingredient: its darker color, tart fruitiness, and balsamic depth are more at home in a glaze or sauce than on a stack of pancakes.[^2]

The tree itself also matters to the flavor story. Birch sap contains more mineral material than maple sap, and the final syrup concentrates that material along with the sugars, which is why producers filter it carefully.[^2] The result is not the minty profile associated with birch beer; it is a cooked tree sap whose identity comes from fructose-rich caramelization and acidity.[^2][^3]
