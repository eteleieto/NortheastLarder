*Capsicum annuum* ‘Hungarian Wax’

Hungarian wax is a long, tapered pepper that begins pale yellow and ripens through orange to red. It resembles a banana pepper, but many strains carry appreciable chile heat.[^1]


Like other peppers it is a frost-tender crop, but its relatively early maturity lets it fit a Northeast growing season when plants are started ahead. The pale fruit is not necessarily unripe in the everyday market sense: yellow is a normal harvest stage, while further ripening changes color and flavor.[^2]

Peppers need warm soil and a long enough run of warm days, which is why Maine growers generally start them as transplants and choose early varieties in the northern part of the state.[^2][^3] The fruit is botanically a berry, with a hollow interior divided by pale ribs that carry much of the heat-producing chemistry; removing those ribs and seeds can make a hot fruit taste gentler, though it does not guarantee a mild pepper.

“Wax” refers to the smooth, waxy-looking skin and the yellow-wax pepper type, not to a coating added after harvest. Heat can vary between seed strains and individual fruits because capsaicinoid production responds to genetics and growing conditions, so the name describes a cultivar family more reliably than an exact Scoville number. As the fruit ripens from yellow toward orange and red, its aroma becomes fruitier and its flesh softer while the green, grassy notes fade.[^1]


Hungarian wax and [[Santa Fe Pepper|Santa Fe]] peppers are both yellow-wax forms of *C. annuum*. Their similarity is visual as well as culinary, but they come from different regional breeding histories.
