*Phaseolus vulgaris*

Green beans are crisp, tender pods, including green and yellow wax types. They are an easy Northeast garden crop and a familiar summer preserve.[^1]


Green snap beans, wax beans, pole beans, shell beans, and dry beans are all forms of the same species, *Phaseolus vulgaris*; the difference is largely the stage and part harvested.[^2]

The string in older “string beans” is a tough fiber running along the pod seam. Modern stringless cultivars were selected to reduce it. A snap bean is harvested before the seeds and pod wall fully mature; leave the same kind of plant longer and the pod eventually becomes papery around dry beans.[^2]

Round, slender pods and broad, flat pods are the two general forms; flat varieties tend to have a more intense flavor. A useful harvest test is the seed inside: snap beans are ready while the pod is succulent and the individual seeds have not yet begun to bulge visibly.[^4] That narrow stage is why the same plant can supply a tender vegetable one week and a shell or dry bean later in the season.

Cooked green-bean aroma is unusually complex, combining sulfurous and freshly green compounds with mushroom-like octenol and floral linalool.[^3] The pod wall contributes most of the crunch, while the immature seeds add a soft, starchy center; as the pod matures, the wall becomes more fibrous and the seeds take over. Once harvested, the pods respire rapidly, consume their sugars, lose moisture, and become less sweet and crisp even in cold storage.[^3] That quick decline explains the green bean's strong Northeast identity as a summer garden crop and a classic candidate for preserving soon after picking.[^1]
