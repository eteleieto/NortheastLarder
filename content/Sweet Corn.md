*Zea mays* var. *saccharata*

Sweet corn is a high-summer crop with notably sugary kernels. Its sweetness begins converting to starch after harvest, which is why local corn tastes so different from corn that has traveled.[^1]

Corn, beans, and squash were the principal crops of Native American farmers in the Northeast for centuries before Europeans arrived; they are often called the Three Sisters.[^2]

Fresh corn's mouthfeel comes from three carbohydrate pools: sugars supply sweetness, dissolved medium-length polysaccharides supply creaminess, and starch supplies dry density. Traditional sweet corn carries more of the creamy polysaccharides than supersweet types, which keep their sugars longer after harvest but can taste comparatively watery.[^3]

Sweet corn is picked immature rather than dried on the stalk like field corn. The ideal window is short: the kernels should still be tender and milky, and once the ears are off the plant, warmth quickly turns sugar into starch. That is why the difference between corn eaten the same day and corn that sat around the market for a while can be so dramatic.[^4][^5]

In the Northeast, that perishability is part of the point. Sweet corn is one of the clearest arguments for local summer produce, because refrigeration and fast handling preserve a quality that shipping can only partly protect. The same ear can read as creamy and almost custardy when fresh, then much flatter and starchier a day later.[^1][^3][^5]
