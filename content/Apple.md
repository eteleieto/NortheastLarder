*Malus domestica*

Apple is a temperate tree fruit with deep roots in Northeast orchards, where growers care as much about acidity, tannin, texture, and storage life as simple sweetness.[^1] The cultivated apple is a pome selected from a broader wild apple complex, and its modern spread depended on grafting: seed-grown apples vary wildly, so the named fruit people care about are really clones carried forward by hand.[^2]

That variability is part of why apples stay so useful in a notebook like this one. A firm, sharp cooking apple, a perfumed dessert apple, and a tannic cider apple can all be the same species while behaving like different ingredients. Maine Extension notes how much northern growers still care about winter hardiness and ripening time, while cool nights help the fruit hold acidity and color.[^1]

The fruit itself is built differently from denser pome relatives like pears. McGee notes that apples can be as much as a quarter air by volume, with open spaces between cells that help make a fresh apple crisp.[^3] The same structure also explains two familiar failures: when the cells soften and dry, the fruit turns mealy, and when a whole apple bakes, those air pockets fill with steam and can split the skin.[^3]

Apples are also naturally high in pectin, which is why they set well in jelly and give even plain sauce a thick, satisfying body.[^3] Cut flesh browns when damaged cells bring phenolic compounds, enzymes, and oxygen together, so acid and quick heat both slow the reaction in different ways.[^4] That combination of perfume, acid, structure, and pectin is what makes apples so flexible across the Northeast, whether the fruit ends up fresh, cooked down, dried, or fermented into cider.[^2][^3]
