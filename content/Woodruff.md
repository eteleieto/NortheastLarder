*Galium odoratum*

Sweet woodruff is a low, shade-tolerant perennial with whorled leaves and small white flowers. Its characteristic hay-and-vanilla aroma becomes more apparent as the leaves wilt or dry.[^1] It is not native to the Northeast, but its hardiness and tolerance of partial to full shade make it a realistic cultivated herb for woodland gardens here.[^2]


The aroma is associated with coumarin, the same broad flavor compound family that gives tonka bean and fresh-cut hay some of their character. This is a small-quantity flavoring plant, not a bulk herb.[^1]


Sweet woodruff spreads by rhizomes and can form a continuous woodland groundcover. Its square stems and whorled leaves are useful identification clues, while the white flowers appear in spring above the low foliage.[^2] In Germany it is strongly associated with *Waldmeister* flavoring, especially around May Day.[^2]

Fresh leaves are comparatively quiet; the hay, almond, and vanilla character develops as the plant wilts or dries. That makes woodruff unusual among herbs in the Northeast: its most recognizable food aroma is created after harvest, and a small amount can perfume an infusion without behaving like a conventional leafy seasoning.[^2][^3]

The plant prefers shade and can spread vigorously once established. It is therefore better understood as a cultivated woodland herb than as a casual roadside forage, especially because correct identification matters for any plant gathered for food.[^3][^4] The delay between harvest and aroma is the memorable food fact: freshly cut woodruff can seem almost scentless, while drying concentrates a hay-, almond-, and vanilla-like character. That makes it closer to a flavoring material than a conventional leafy green.[^2]
