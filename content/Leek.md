*Allium porrum*

Leeks are the gentler, sweeter allium, with white and pale-green layered stalks. Their mildness comes between onion and garlic, without either ingredient’s sharpest edge.[^1]


Leeks are alliums grown for long, layered leaf bases rather than a round bulb. Blanching the lower stem keeps that portion pale and tender.[^1]


Leeks need a long head start in Maine: Extension lists them at 10–12 weeks indoors before planting out in mid-to-late spring.[^2]

Leeks are often planted in a trench and gradually earthed up as they grow, excluding light from the lower stem and extending the tender white portion.[^4] That pale section is not a different organ from the green leaves; it is the same tightly wrapped leaf bases, simply grown without much light. The whole plant is edible, although the dark green leaves are tougher and more cabbage-like than the white base.

The inner leaves have the strongest allium flavor. Leeks also contain fructans and other long-chain carbohydrates that help give cooked leek a slippery texture and can make a chilled preparation gel slightly as it cools.[^3] Their sulfur compounds are milder than onion’s, so heat brings out sweetness without erasing the plant’s savory allium character.

Leeks are biennial, but the kitchen usually meets them during the first year, before the plant sends up its tall flowering stalk. Left to flower, a leek becomes architectural as well as edible, with a round cluster of flowers that can produce seed for the next crop. That long cycle helps explain why the vegetable feels more like a carefully managed stalk than a quick bulb crop.
