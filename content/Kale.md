*Brassica oleracea* var. *sabellica*

Kale is a sturdy, slightly bitter brassica green. It tolerates cool Northeast conditions and remains structurally leafy rather than forming the dense head seen in [[Cabbage|cabbage]].[^1] In New England that is not just a gardening convenience: kale belongs to the cool-season group that can be planted for spring or fall harvest, when warmer crops are unreliable.[^7]


Kale contains glucosinolates, sulfur-rich plant compounds characteristic of the brassica family.[^2]


Kale is one of the non-heading forms of *Brassica oleracea*: a species also selected into cabbage, [[Broccoli|broccoli]], [[Cauliflower|cauliflower]], and [[Brussels Sprouts|brussels sprouts]]. The wild ancestor is a seaside-cliff plant of the Mediterranean and southwestern Europe, and the leafy, non-heading forms that became kale were selected for edible leaves rather than heads.[^3][^4]

The Northeast's cold season is where kale earns its reputation. UMass Extension's Cold-Hardy Greens Project has spent several winters at its South Deerfield research farm selecting brassica greens suited to cold-climate New England, and Vermont growers report picking kale from open fields and tunnels through the winter.[^5][^6] University of Maine guidance likewise treats kale as a dependable northern garden crop, while recent Maine advice notes that cooling nights can improve the flavor of fall brassicas.[^7][^8] The leaf’s toughness is therefore part of its regional usefulness: it can remain harvestable after more delicate greens have passed, and its firm texture stands up to storage and cooking.
