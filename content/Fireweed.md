*Chamaenerion angustifolium* (also *Epilobium angustifolium*)

Fireweed is a tall northern perennial associated with roadsides, clearings, river edges, and recently burned ground. Its name comes from its habit of colonizing burned-over areas, and in Maine it is a familiar midsummer plant with a tall spike of deep pink flowers.[^1][^3]


Young shoots, leaves, unopened buds, and flowers all appear in northern food traditions, but the plant changes quickly as it matures: older foliage becomes tougher and more bitter. The first shoots are often compared with asparagus, while the young leaves are mild enough for greens or a light tea. Native Plant Trust records the young leaves, shoots, and flowers as edible and notes the flowers’ use in fireweed jelly, while also documenting the plant across disturbed New England habitats.[^2][^5] Once flowering is well underway, older blossoms become bitter, so the edible window is part of the ingredient's character rather than a minor harvest detail.


Its flowers open progressively up the stem rather than all at once, moving upward as the season advances. Maine’s flowering period is generally July into August, and the flowers draw bumblebees, hummingbirds, and hummingbird moths.[^1] Its extensive root system helps it recolonize disturbed ground, but it can also spread aggressively where it is cultivated.[^4] The flower spike is a sequential structure rather than a single flush: flowers open from the lower part upward, leaving a changing mix of buds, open flowers, and spent seed capsules on one stem. That gives fireweed several different textures over a short harvest period and makes timing central to its use as food.
