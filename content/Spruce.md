*Picea* spp.

Spruce tips are the soft, pale new shoots at the ends of branches. At that stage their flavor is brighter and less woody than mature needles, often reading as resin, citrus peel, and green fruit.


Red, white, and black spruce all occur in Maine, and spruce-fir forest is especially characteristic of the state’s cooler coast and north. Spruce needles are short, sharp, and attached singly rather than in the bundles seen on [[Pine|pine]]. The genus *Picea* holds its needles on small peg-like projections, a structural detail that helps distinguish it from other Northeast evergreens.[^1]


Young spruce growth has a long food and drink history in northern regions. Newly grown tips have been used to flavor spruce beer, and National Park Service documentation of Wabanaki plant use records white spruce bark, twigs, cones, and roots in food and beverage contexts.[^2] In modern Northeast cooking, the tender tips are valued for their resinous, citrus-like aroma, which is softer and brighter before the needles harden.


The common field test for a spruce needle is that it rolls between the fingers: unlike a flat fir or hemlock needle, it is roughly four-sided. Careful identification still matters around any wild needled evergreen.
