*Sambucus canadensis* (American elder)

American elder is a native Northeast shrub with flat-topped clusters of flowers followed by drooping clusters of deep purple to black berries on colored stems. It commonly occurs in wet ground, marshes, stream edges, and disturbed sites, and tolerates both flooding and shade.[^1]

The name elderberry covers several species and several gathered plant parts. Accurate identification matters, and the fruit is usually processed rather than eaten raw.[^1][^3] The berries are tart enough that sugar is often part of the finished food, but their aroma and dark anthocyanin color make them more than a sweetener carrier. The flowers are a separate seasonal ingredient, appearing before the fruit and forming broad, pale clusters that are easy to distinguish from the later berries.

Cooking is more than a matter of taming tartness: elderberries contain lectins that heat inactivates. Their dark color comes from abundant anthocyanin pigments, which also readily color syrups, wines, and preserves.[^2]


American elder is native to Maine and supports many bird species, which is part of why fruit harvest can be competitive in the landscape.[^1] The plant's usefulness as both a native wildlife shrub and a food-bearing perennial is characteristic of elder: a patch can belong to the ecology of a wet field edge while also supplying a strongly flavored fruit for the kitchen.
