*Elaeagnus umbellata*

Autumn olive is a foraged fruit in the Northeast, but it comes with the complication that the shrub is aggressively invasive in New England and spreads readily through bird-dispersed seed.[^1][^2] It thrives in roadsides, fields, forest edges, and other open disturbed ground, which is one reason it is so easy to encounter during fruit season.[^1]

The berries ripen red with silver speckling and usually have a sweet-tart flavor that lands somewhere between [[Red Currant|red currant]], [[Cherry|cherry]], and a slightly greener [[Red Raspberry|raspberry]]. The flesh is thin, the single seed is noticeable, and the astringency drops as the fruit softens, which is why the berries are often better fully ripe than merely colored.

For kitchen purposes, the fruit is more serious than the shrub's hedge-row reputation suggests. USDA work found that red autumn olive fruit can carry notably high lycopene levels, often well above fresh tomatoes, and described the berries as suitable for preserves, condiments, juice, flavoring, and other products.[^3] In Connecticut guidance, the berries are specifically noted as abundant red fruit in September and October, which matches the narrow Northeast window when the plant becomes worth foraging rather than just cursing.[^2]
