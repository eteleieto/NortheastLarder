*Lentinula edodes*

Shiitake is a cultivated wood-decaying mushroom valued less for delicacy than for how much savory depth it can generate in a small amount. It fits the Northeast cleanly because log-grown shiitake has become an established specialty crop for northeastern growers, especially on hardwood bolts, and because the mushroom's drying, roasting, and fermenting behavior makes it more useful in this notebook than a generic supermarket mushroom.[^1][^2]

McGee notes that shiitake cultivation on oak logs goes back to China in the thirteenth century, which matters here because that log-grown form is still the model for much Northeastern production.[^2] The Cornell-UVM shiitake manual was written specifically for the northeastern United States and treats local tree species, laying yards, and markets as a distinct regional system rather than a copy of southern production.[^1]

Food-wise, shiitake matters because it carries both free glutamates and the nucleotide GMP, one of the compounds that deepens umami when it meets glutamate-rich foods.[^3] McGee also points out that the mushroom's characteristic aroma comes from sulfur chemistry, especially lenthionine, and that drying and rehydrating intensify those savory notes rather than flattening them.[^3] That makes shiitake unusually good at moving between fresh mushroom, dried pantry ingredient, stock base, powder, and ferment substrate.

That is exactly how it shows up here. [[Lacto-Fermented Shiitake]] uses the mushroom as a preservation project and a way to capture savory brine, while [[Oyster Mushroom]] already treats shiitake as the denser, more umami-heavy comparison point.
