---
title: Rose Hip
aliases: [rose hips]
tags: [INGREDIENT]
---

*Rosa* spp.

Rose hips are the fruits that remain after rose flowers are pollinated. Depending on species, they range from small and firm to the large orange-red hips of beach rose.[^1] They are not berries in the botanical sense: the fleshy cup is a swollen flower structure that encloses many small, dry fruits, each with its own seed.[^2]


The fleshy outer part is tart and aromatic. Inside are hard seeds surrounded by irritating hairs, which need to be removed or carefully strained out.[^2] That inner architecture explains why the hip is usually turned into a strained preserve or infusion rather than eaten whole. The fruit’s natural pectin also helps it set into the jellies and preserves for which hips are traditionally known.[^3]


Beach rose (*Rosa rugosa*) is common along the Maine coast but is introduced and can spread aggressively. Its hips may be locally abundant even though the plant is not native. UMaine describes the orange-to-red fruits as useful for teas, jams, and jellies, while also noting the shrub’s role in dune stabilization and its invasive status in parts of the Northeast.[^1]

The fruit’s tartness and perfume can vary widely among roses, from bright and almost citrus-like to darker and more resinous. That variation, along with the need to separate the hairs and seeds, makes rose hip a useful ingredient for concentrated preserves and infusions rather than a standardized raw fruit.
