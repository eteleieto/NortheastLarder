*Fagopyrum esculentum*

Buckwheat is not a wheat or even a grass. It is a broadleaf member of the knotweed family, making it a relative of [[Rhubarb|rhubarb]] and [[Japanese Knotweed|Japanese knotweed]].[^1]

Buckwheat fits Maine’s short season unusually well: UMaine describes a roughly twelve-week crop, commonly sown in late June or early July and ripening around early October.[^1]

The living plant and its residue can suppress some weed germination through allelopathy. Its fast canopy also physically shades weeds, so the crop works through more than one mechanism.[^1]

Its flowers support a strongly flavored [[Buckwheat Honey|buckwheat honey]]. The dark color and malty flavor come from the bees' nectar source, not from ground buckwheat mixed into the finished honey.

The triangular kernel has a dark hull around a starchy endosperm; once the hull is removed, the intact seed is a groat. Buckwheat contains more oil than most cereals, which shortens the shelf life of both groats and flour, and phenolics contribute a slight astringency.[^2] Its cooked aroma combines nutty pyrazines with smoky, green, and faintly fishy notes, while a small amount of water-binding mucilage supplies some of the cohesion in all-buckwheat noodles.[^2]

[^1]: University of Maine Cooperative Extension, “[Maine Forage Facts: Small Grains Crops](https://extension.umaine.edu/publications/2270e/),” accessed July 17, 2026.
[^2]: Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen*, rev. ed. (Scribner, 2004), PDF p. 497.
