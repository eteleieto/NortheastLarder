*Cucurbita pepo*

Zucchini is a tender summer squash, usually harvested young while its skin is thin and its seeds are small. It is productive in Northeast gardens and shares a plant with edible squash blossoms.[^1]


The plant’s [[Squash Blossom|blossoms]] are edible too. Zucchini and many pumpkins are cultivars of *Cucurbita pepo*, even though their mature fruits look quite different.


Zucchini is a warm-season cucurbit; UMaine groups it with tomatoes and cucumbers among fruiting crops that need at least eight hours of direct sun for good production.[^2]

That warm-climate ancestry still matters after harvest. Summer squash suffers chilling injury at ordinary refrigerator temperatures; cold can damage membranes even before visible decay, so “colder” does not always mean “better keeping” for zucchini.[^3] The fruit is mostly water, and its mild flavor depends on harvesting before the seeds enlarge and the rind toughens. UMaine’s home-garden guidance recommends picking summer squash young, when the skin is still easily penetrated and the flesh remains firm rather than stringy.[^1][^4]

Botanically, zucchini is an immature fruit rather than a root or stem. The swollen fruit develops from a female flower, and the seeds inside are still soft enough to eat. That gives the plant two overlapping food stages: the tender fruit and the blossoms that precede it. Left on the vine, the same fruit becomes a large, hard-skinned squash with a different texture and storage life.

The plant’s abundance is part of its Northeast character. Zucchini can begin producing quickly once warm soil and frost-free weather arrive, but its broad leaves and soft fruit also invite cucumber beetles, squash bugs, and disease. A good regional harvest therefore comes in a rush, with the crop’s best texture lasting only briefly before the fruit turns oversized.
