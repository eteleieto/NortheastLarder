*Fagopyrum esculentum*

Buckwheat is not a wheat or even a grass. It is a fast-growing annual in the knotweed family, which makes it a relative of [[Rhubarb|rhubarb]] and [[Japanese Knotweed|Japanese knotweed]] rather than of the cereal grains it often stands beside.[^1][^2] That botanical mismatch shows up in the kitchen too: buckwheat behaves grain-like, but it has its own flavor, protein balance, and texture.

It also makes practical sense in the Northeast. UMaine describes it as a roughly twelve-week crop commonly sown in late June or early July and harvested around early October, and Arthur Haines records garden buckwheat as present across all six New England states in and around cultivation.[^1][^3] The plant comes up quickly, tolerates poor soils, and shades the ground fast enough to suppress weeds, which is part of why it keeps reappearing in regional farming conversations.[^1][^2]

The dark, strongly flavored [[Buckwheat Honey|buckwheat honey]] associated with the crop comes from its flowers, not from anything mixed into the finished honey. The seeds themselves are angular fruits with a dark hull around a pale starchy interior; once dehulled, the intact seed is a groat.[^2][^4] Vaughan and Geissler note that the grain is relatively rich in lysine compared with true cereals, while McGee points out that its higher oil content and phenolics can shorten shelf life and contribute a slight astringency.[^2][^4]

Buckwheat's flavor is nuttier and more assertive than most mild cereals, which is why it can stand up to roasting, honey, or fermentation without disappearing. McGee describes cooked buckwheat as carrying pyrazine-driven nutty notes along with smoky, green, and faintly fishy ones, and he also notes that a small amount of mucilage helps give all-buckwheat noodles some cohesion despite the lack of gluten.[^2] That combination makes buckwheat especially useful when the goal is a grain that tastes like itself rather than merely serving as neutral starch.
