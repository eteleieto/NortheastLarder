*Secale cereale*

Rye has a darker, earthier flavor than [[Wheat|wheat]] and a gentle bitter edge. It is often associated with bread and whiskey, but in the Northeast it also matters as a cover crop, a milling grain, and a base for drinks like [[Toasted Rye Tea]].[^1]

Rye seems to have started as a weed moving through early [[Wheat|wheat]] and [[Barley|barley]] fields in southwest Asia before becoming a crop in its own right. It proved especially useful in cool, poor, acidic ground, which helps explain why it became such a durable bread grain across northern Europe and why it still fits northern grain systems so well.[^2][^3] University of Maine’s winter rye trial report describes the crop as a long-standing Maine cover crop and notes that regional markets now include seed, flour, and beverage uses in addition to agronomic ones.[^1]

In the kitchen, rye behaves differently from wheat because its structure leans less on a stretchy gluten network and more on water-holding arabinoxylans, the carbohydrates older food writing often calls pentosans.[^2][^4] Those gums help rye doughs stay cohesive, help sourdough-style rye loaves keep moisture for a long time, and make pure rye breads rely on acidity and long baking rather than on big airy lift.[^4] That same grain can also move in a very different direction once it is toasted, malted, or fermented, which is why it appears here in [[Rye Amazake]], [[Rye Koji "Coffee"]], and [[Toasted Rye Tea]].
