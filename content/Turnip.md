*Brassica rapa* subsp. *rapa*

Turnips are crisp and peppery when young, becoming sweeter and more earthy as they mature. Their roots and greens are both edible, and they are well suited to cool Northeast growing conditions.[^1]


Turnip’s brassica bite comes partly from glucosinolates, sulfur-rich defense compounds found across the mustard family.[^2]


Turnips are another crop that can be planted both early and in late summer in Maine, a reflection of their cool-weather tolerance.[^3]

The edible swelling combines lower stem and taproot rather than being a root alone. It stores little starch compared with a potato and therefore cooks relatively quickly while retaining a moist texture. Prolonged cooking eventually makes the flesh mushy and lets cabbage-like sulfur aromas dominate.[^4]

That two-part identity matters at the table: the root is mild enough to eat young and crisp, while the greens are stronger and more assertive. Maine Extension groups turnip greens with the stronger-flavored brassica greens and notes that blanching can soften their bitter or sulfurous edge.[^5] The crop is also unusually useful in the Northeast because it tolerates the shoulder seasons; a late planting can mature while the garden is moving away from more heat-loving vegetables.[^3]

Turnip flavor is not fixed by variety alone. Glucosinolate profiles change with tissue, cultivar, growth stage, and growing conditions, so a young root, an older root, and the leaves can have noticeably different peppery intensity even on the same plant.[^6] That variation is part of why turnips can read as a fresh radish-like vegetable when small and as a dense, earthy winter root when allowed to size up.
