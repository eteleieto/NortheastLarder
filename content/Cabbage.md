*Brassica oleracea* var. *capitata*

Cabbage is a long-keeping Northeast vegetable with a mild, mineral-sweet character. Dense heads give it good storage life after harvest.[^1]

That storage habit is part of why cabbage stays useful in northern kitchens. Maine planting guidance treats it as both an early-season transplant and a crop for late-summer planting, which fits its preference for cool weather and helps explain why it shows up both fresh and preserved over a long stretch of the year.[^2]

Like other brassicas, cabbage contains glucosinolates: sulfur-rich plant compounds involved in the plant’s chemical defense system.[^3] McGee notes that the distribution is uneven inside the plant, with young actively growing tissues and the core running more intense than the outer leaves, and that growing conditions can shift the balance as well.[^4]

Cutting cabbage brings those flavor precursors into contact with the enzymes that transform them, increasing pungency before cooking even begins. Plunging cabbage into abundant boiling water inactivates the enzymes quickly and produces a milder result; prolonged heat eventually generates the trisulfides responsible for the lingering smell of overcooked cabbage.[^4]


Fermentation changes the same chemistry in a different direction. Steinkraus notes that during sauerkraut fermentation many bitter and sulfurous cabbage compounds are masked or altered, while cabbage itself seems to contribute some heat-sensitive bactericidal activity that helps push back undesirable bacteria early in the process.[^5] That makes cabbage one of the clearest examples of an ingredient whose raw, cooked, and fermented versions feel like related but genuinely different foods.
