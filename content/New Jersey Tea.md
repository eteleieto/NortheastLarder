*Ceanothus americanus*

New Jersey tea is a small deciduous shrub native across New England, including all six states. It has unusually deep roots, fixes nitrogen through a bacterial partnership, and is adapted to persist after fire.[^1][^3]


The common name refers to the historical use of its astringent leaves as a colonial tea substitute. A 1768 Boston newspaper even described Maine-grown material offered as an alternative to imported Bohea tea.[^2] It is not related to tea, *Camellia sinensis*; the resemblance is in the brewed use, not the botany. The leaves' astringency gives the drink more structure than a neutral herbal infusion.


New Jersey tea favors dry, sandy or rocky ground and can be slow to establish while it builds its root system. Its white flowers arrive in dense midsummer clusters, and the shrub stays relatively small—usually under three feet—so it belongs as much to a sunny field edge or rocky garden as to a beverage history.[^3] The deep red roots explain the older name “redroot,” but the leaves are the part associated with the tea substitute.

The plant's food identity is mostly historical and aromatic rather than nutritional. The leaves contain tannins, which give an infusion a drying, structured finish, but they contain no caffeine because New Jersey tea is a ceanothus, not a tea plant.[^2][^4] In the Northeast that distinction matters: a locally gathered or cultivated leaf could stand in for imported tea during wartime, yet it was never botanically related to the commercial tea trade it was replacing.

New Jersey tea also behaves like a disturbance-adapted shrub. Its deep woody root crown can resprout after fire, and its nitrogen-fixing partnership helps it persist on poor, dry ground.[^1][^5] The white flowers and small dry fruits make it ecologically useful beyond the cup, while the leaves remain the plant's clearest food connection. It is therefore best understood as a regional tea substitute with a strong botanical story, not as a caffeine-free version of *Camellia sinensis*.
