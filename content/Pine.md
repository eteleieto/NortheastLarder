*Pinus* spp.; Northeast food use most often points to eastern white pine, *Pinus strobus*

Eastern white pine needles have a resinous, citrus-leaning aroma and a long regional association with needle infusions. The growing tips, or “candles,” are the tree’s soft new stems before their needles spread out.[^1]

Eastern white pine is native throughout New England and grows quickly in the cool, humid climate of the region. It can become a very large, long-lived tree, but the food-relevant harvest is usually the aromatic new growth rather than the mature timber-sized plant. A handy identification clue is that its needles grow five to a bundle—the same number of letters as “white.”[^2]

The five-needle bundles matter because “pine” is not a safe enough identification for gathering. Eastern hemlock, spruce, fir, and yew can all look broadly coniferous, but their needle arrangement and chemistry differ; yew is especially dangerous. Once the species is confirmed, the young needles contribute a bright resinous aroma, while the softer spring candles are less fibrous than older growth.[^1][^3]


“Needled evergreen” is not a food identity. Yew (*Taxus*) can be mistaken for other evergreens and contains highly poisonous taxine compounds in its needles and most other tissues. Any gathered conifer ingredient needs species-level identification.[^3]


Pine and [[Spruce|spruce]] share resinous evergreen aromas, but their needles are arranged differently: pine needles come in bundles, while spruce needles attach one at a time.
