---
title: Lamb's Quarters
tags: [INGREDIENT]
---

*Chenopodium album*

Lamb's-quarters is a common wild green that shows up in Northeast gardens, field edges, and other disturbed ground, where it is usually gathered young and cooked like spinach.[^1][^2] It is one of those volunteer plants that reads as a weed in one context and as dinner in another.

The leaves are mild enough to mix with stronger greens, and UMaine's wild-greens guide notes that the younger shoots are the ones worth seeking out.[^1] The pale, mealy coating on the foliage is more than a visual quirk: it is one of the easiest field marks for separating lamb's-quarters from the saltier coastal [[Orache|orache]] that appears in the same guide.[^1]

Botanically it is white goosefoot, *Chenopodium album*, and Arthur Haines records it as widespread across all six New England states in roadsides, gardens, cultivated fields, and waste areas.[^2] Vaughan and Geissler also place it in the old goosefoot family alongside orache and Good King Henry, all of them part of a long tradition of useful greens that sit just outside the standard market-vegetable canon.[^3] That family relationship helps explain why lamb's-quarters feels familiar on the plate: it has the mineral, spinach-adjacent quality of a domesticated leaf crop, but it still behaves like a volunteer.

The plant is an annual, so its persistence comes from seed rather than a perennial root system. Its lower leaves may be toothed or lobed while the upper leaves are more entire, and the tiny green flowers are carried in clustered spikes; the mealy coating is variable, which is why identification should not rest on that one feature alone.[^4] The mature seeds have also been used as a grain, although their hard coats make them a different food from the tender summer greens.[^1][^4]
