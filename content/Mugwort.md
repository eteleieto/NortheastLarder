*Artemisia vulgaris*

Mugwort is a strongly aromatic perennial in the daisy family. It is established throughout the Northeast and spreads through deep rhizomes, which is why a plant that can be gathered as an herb can also become a serious garden and nursery weed.[^1][^2]

Its culinary identity is closer to a bitter, resinous seasoning than to a tender leafy green. Mugwort is used in Japanese and Chinese cooking, but university extension specialists caution against treating the common Northeast plant as a bulk vegetable or planting it freely in the ground.[^3] That tension matters here: mugwort is interesting as a local aromatic and fermentation ingredient, but its food use needs careful identification, restrained quantities, and attention to where it was gathered.

The plant’s deep roots and spreading habit also explain why it persists in fields and disturbed ground. Its alternate leaves are deeply lobed lower on the stem and narrower above, with a gray-white underside and a chrysanthemum- or sage-like aroma when rubbed.[^2] Those traits help separate it from other aromatic roadside plants, but they are not a substitute for experienced identification.

In Northeast food work, mugwort is a gathered flavor with a distinctly different agricultural story from a cultivated tea herb: it is abundant in some places, but abundance does not remove the need for control or caution. UConn records common mugwort as an introduced perennial now widespread through the region, arriving in North America centuries ago and spreading through disturbed habitats and nursery stock.[^2] Its culinary history is therefore layered onto an invasive-plant problem rather than a native wild-food tradition.
