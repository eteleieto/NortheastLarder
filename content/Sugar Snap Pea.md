*Pisum sativum*

Sugar snap peas are edible-pod peas bred for a thick, crisp pod that can be eaten while the seeds inside are nearly mature. They sit between shelling peas and snow peas: the pod is fuller and crunchier than a snow pea, while the young seeds are still sweet and tender.[^1]

Peas are especially well suited to the Northeast’s cool spring. University of Minnesota Extension gives 55–65°F as a good growing range and recommends planting as soon as the soil is workable; sugar snaps can then be harvested when the pod is filled but still juicy. The vines, tender shoot tips, and tendrils are edible too, giving the plant more food value than the familiar pod alone.[^1] That cool-season preference also explains why a hot summer can end a pea planting quickly: flowering and pod quality fall away as the crop moves past its preferred temperatures.

Their sweetness is fleeting. Once picked, peas lose moisture and sugars quickly, and over-mature pods become starchy with tougher skins.[^2] Minnesota Extension recommends sampling the pods frequently and cooling them soon after harvest; the ideal sugar snap is full enough to show rounded peas while the pod remains tender and juicy.[^1] That short window is part of what makes sugar snaps useful in a regional food system: they reward eating close to harvest and preserve a clear sense of early-summer freshness in preparations such as the notebook’s lacto-fermented snap peas.

The snap pea’s defining texture comes from its pod walls. Unlike a shelling pea, whose mature pod becomes fibrous, a sugar snap is selected for a thick, fleshy pod that stays edible while the seeds swell inside; unlike a snow pea, it is round rather than flat and carries more developed peas.[^3] That makes the pod a combined vegetable and seed container, with crispness from the fresh wall and sweetness from the developing seeds.

In the Northeast, the crop’s schedule is governed by cool weather. Maine Extension recommends sowing peas as soon as the ground is workable, with soil around 45°F, and notes that they can also be tried as a fall crop if an early, disease-resistant variety is sown well before frost.[^4] Vining snap peas may climb six feet or more, while bush types need much less support; either way, the crop fits naturally into the short spring window before heat reduces flowering and pod quality.[^5]
