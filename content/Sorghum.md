*Sorghum bicolor*

Sorghum is a warm-season grass with distinct grain, sweet-stem, and forage types. Grain sorghum is often called milo; sweet sorghum is grown for the sugary juice in its stalks. That split matters because one plant can point either toward porridge and fermentation or toward syrup and molasses-like sweeteners.[^1][^2]

The crop originated in central and southern Africa, spread early into India and China, and became important wherever heat, drought, or poor soils ruled out fussier cereals.[^3] McGee notes that the grain is boiled, popped, milled into porridges and flatbreads, and fermented into beer across warm dry regions.[^3] In the American context, sweet sorghum also developed a strong syrup tradition: Deborah Jean Warner describes how nineteenth-century growers treated it as a practical northern and western alternative to cane sugar, especially for home syrup production.[^2]

That history only partly overlaps with the Northeast. UMaine treats sorghum as a genuinely warm-season crop and recommends waiting until soils exceed about 65°F, usually around early June, before planting.[^1] In other words, it can be grown here, but it does not behave like [[Oat|oats]], [[Rye|rye]], or [[Barley|barley]]. Part of the point of using it in this notebook is precisely that tension between a heat-loving grain and a colder regional pantry.

Sorghum also has one memorable bit of food chemistry attached to it. Young, stressed, or germinating material can build or activate dhurrin, part of a cyanide-generating defense system.[^1][^3] That is mostly a forage-management warning rather than an everyday kitchen hazard, but it does explain why sorghum is a poor candidate for the kind of sprouting that works well with other grains. Even as a dry grain, though, it brings something useful: a sturdy, slightly mineral flavor and a structure that often benefits from cracking, milling, or other processing before enzymes can really get to work.

The grain is a caryopsis like [[Corn|corn]] or [[Wheat|wheat]], but the seed stays relatively small and hard, with a substantial outer layer that can contribute color, tannin, and astringency. Some food sorghums are pale and mild; others are red, brown, or nearly black, and their phenolic compounds can make the cooked grain taste more assertive. Milling, pearling, popping, and fermentation all change how much of that outer character reaches the finished food.[^3][^4]

For the Northeast, sorghum is best understood as a possible warm-season specialty crop rather than a peer of winter rye or barley. Maine Extension distinguishes grain, sweet, forage, and hybrid sorghums and notes that the crop needs warm, well-drained conditions; sweet sorghum’s syrup tradition is especially interesting because the stalk, not the seed, is the main sugar-bearing part.[^1][^5] That split between grain and stalk gives the plant two very different culinary identities while keeping both within the same regional growing experiment.
