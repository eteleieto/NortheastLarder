*Calendula officinalis*

Calendula petals bring warm yellow-orange color and a lightly tangy, peppery note. They are sometimes called “poor man’s saffron” for their color rather than for matching saffron’s flavor.[^1]


Only the ray florets—the petal-like outer parts of the flower—are generally used as the edible portion.[^2]

Calendula is also called pot marigold, but it is not a *Tagetes* marigold. The apparent flower is a composite: one head packed with many small florets, of which the outer ray florets are the petal-like parts usually scattered through food.[^2] In a Northeast garden it behaves as an easy annual, and its orange or yellow flowers are useful precisely because they bring both color and a peppery edge rather than the heavy perfume of some edible flowers.[^1][^3]

The plant’s “saffron” reputation is mostly about visual effect. Calendula can tint food warm yellow-orange, but its flavor is tangy and peppery, so it works best as a small accent. Maine Extension specifically recommends growing edible flowers without pesticides and washing them before eating, which matters more for calendula than the plant's cheerful appearance suggests.[^3][^4]
