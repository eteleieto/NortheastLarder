*Sambucus canadensis* (American elder)

American elder is a native Northeast shrub with flat-topped clusters of flowers followed by dark berries. It commonly occurs along wet ground, stream edges, and roadsides.[^1]


The name elderberry covers several species and several gathered plant parts. Accurate identification matters, and the fruit is usually processed rather than eaten raw.[^1]

Cooking is more than a matter of taming tartness: elderberries contain lectins that heat inactivates. Their dark color comes from abundant anthocyanin pigments, which also readily color syrups, wines, and preserves.[^2]


American elder is native to Maine and supports many bird species, which is part of why fruit harvest can be competitive in the landscape.[^1]

[^1]: University of Maine Cooperative Extension, “[Native Trees and Shrubs for Maine Landscapes: American Elder](https://extension.umaine.edu/publications/2580e/),” accessed July 17, 2026.
[^2]: Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen*, rev. ed. (Scribner, 2004), PDF p. 379.
