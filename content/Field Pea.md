*Pisum sativum*

Field peas are the dried-seed form of the common pea, grown for storage and milling rather than for the sweet green stage. As a food they sit with other pulses: starchy, protein-rich, and better suited to soups, purees, and pastes than to the quick fresh-pea treatment that works for garden peas.[^1][^2]

That split between fresh and dry use matters in the Northeast, where field peas have been part of University of Maine grain-and-pulse trials as both a potential food crop and a practical rotation partner for cereals.[^3] Maine trial notes emphasize the same traits that make them useful in the kitchen and in the field: grain-type peas are shorter, more harvestable plants, and as legumes they can fit into grain systems without the nitrogen demand of a cereal crop.[^3][^4]

The dried seeds keep the dense, mealy character that makes peas such a durable pantry ingredient. They are not as oily as [[Soybean|soybeans]] and they do not eat like fresh [[Pea|peas]] at all; they are closer to dry beans in texture and in the way they reward simmering, pureeing, and fermentation experiments built around starch and protein.[^2][^4]

In Maine, the crop is interesting precisely because it can be both food and field infrastructure. University of Maine trials tested determinate, semi-leafless grain peas alone and mixed with [[Oat|oats]] or [[Barley|barley]]; the cereal companions were meant to help with weeds and lodging, two problems made worse by the Northeast’s humid conditions.[^3][^5] As a legume, the pea also fixes nitrogen with the help of rhizobia, so the harvest is part of a rotation rather than an isolated pulse crop.[^4]

The seed’s useful structure is simple but important: a thin seed coat surrounds two starchy cotyledons, and splitting the dry pea exposes more surface while leaving the characteristic yellow or green color visible. That combination of concentrated starch, moderate protein, and little oil explains why field peas thicken food readily and why they can serve as a quieter alternative to [[Soybean|soybeans]] in regional grain experiments.[^1][^2]
