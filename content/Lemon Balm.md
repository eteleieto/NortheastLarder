*Melissa officinalis*

Lemon balm is a lemon-scented mint-family herb. Its soft leaves have a volatile citrus-herbal aroma that fades with prolonged drying and storage.[^1]


It is related to [[Mint|mint]] and shares the family’s square stems and aromatic oils, but its characteristic note is lemon rather than menthol. In a Northeast garden it behaves as a hardy perennial, preferring sun and reasonably well-drained soil; left alone, it can spread from both seed and creeping stems.[^2]

It is hardy to about USDA Zone 5, which explains why it can persist through much of the Northeast while still behaving like a soft, leafy herb in the kitchen. Pale flowers open above the foliage and attract pollinators; the leaves are the main food, but the flowers are edible too.[^2]

The flowers are edible as well as the leaves, although the leaves carry most of the plant’s culinary identity. Its genus name, *Melissa*, comes from the Greek word for honeybee, a nod to the plant’s attraction for pollinators rather than to any honey in the leaf.[^2]

Its lemon impression is not a single flavor compound but a blend of citrusy and floral terpenes, including citronellal, citronellol, citral, and geraniol. That combination accounts for the leaf's simultaneous lemon, rose-like, and green-herbal qualities.[^3]
