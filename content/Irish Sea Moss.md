*Chondrus crispus*

Irish sea moss is a red seaweed used for its gentle marine flavor and natural thickening power. In the kitchen, it is best known for setting or giving body to puddings, broths, desserts, and other soft preparations.[^1] Its flat, branching fronds grow attached to rocky shorelines, and the red color can shift toward purple, brown, or green depending on light and conditions; it is not the same seaweed as the kelps that dominate Maine’s larger seaweed farms.[^4]

The thickener is carrageenan, a family of sulfated carbohydrates rather than one uniform substance. Different carrageenan types can thicken without setting or form gels ranging from brittle to soft and resilient; potassium and calcium ions strongly influence which networks form as a hot solution cools.[^3] Whole Irish moss therefore behaves less predictably than a standardized commercial carrageenan blend, but its sensitivity to the surrounding liquid is part of its culinary interest.

Rinse it thoroughly and soak it if dried before simmering or blending. A little goes a long way, and its marine flavor works best when considered alongside the desired texture in dairy, fruit, broth, or dessert preparations.


Irish moss is a Maine sea vegetable and is cultured by approved aquaculture facilities in the state. Maine’s Department of Marine Resources lists *Chondrus crispus* among the species available from approved seaweed sources, while UMaine specifically identifies it as a seaweed used in pudding.[^1][^2] That makes Irish moss a useful example of a Northeast ingredient that belongs to both wild coastal ecology and an emerging farmed-seaweed economy.
