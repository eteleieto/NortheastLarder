*Vaccinium macrocarpon*

Cranberries are intensely tart berries native to North America. Their vivid acidity gives them a strong identity among Northeast fruits.[^1]


They share the genus *Vaccinium* with [[Wild Blueberry|wild blueberries]], though cranberries grow on trailing vines in acidic bogs rather than upright lowbush plants.

The plant is a mat of runners with short upright shoots that carry the flowers and fruit. A cranberry bed is wetland-like but not permanently flooded: the roots need a moist, acidic, oxygenated medium, usually sand or peat. That distinction matters in the Northeast, where a bog is a carefully managed growing system rather than simply a pond.[^2]


American cranberry is native to northeastern North America. Cape Cod cultivation began in the early 1800s, and Maine cranberry production was an active industry through much of the twentieth century.[^2]

Maine’s cranberry beds show how a regional crop can be both wild in origin and highly managed in practice. Commercial bogs use a connected system of vines, ditches, ponds, and water controls: winter flooding can insulate the plants, while fall flooding allows ripe berries to float for harvest. Hancock and Washington Counties became the center of Maine’s modern production because their sandy, acidic sites suit the crop.[^2]

Cranberry pollen is too heavy to travel effectively on the wind. Bumblebees and honeybees do most of the crop’s pollination, tying fruit set closely to insect activity during bloom.[^2]

The fruit’s familiar bounce and float are structural clues to its storage and harvest behavior. Air spaces inside the berry help it rise during a water harvest, while its acidity and phenolic compounds slow spoilage. The same chemistry gives cranberry its sharp, almost lemon-like edge and makes its pectin unusually effective at turning a barely cooked puree into a sauce.[^3]

Cranberries keep unusually well for a small berry because their acidity and phenolic compounds suppress spoilage. They are also rich in pectin, so a barely cooked puree can set into a sauce; the same pectin can unexpectedly gel an alcoholic infusion.[^3]
