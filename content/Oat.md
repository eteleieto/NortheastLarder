*Avena sativa*

Oats are a mild, creamy grain that fits the cool, wet Northeast unusually well. In Maine they remain a substantial field crop, and recent UMaine variety work points to a growing interest in food-grade and hulless oats rather than feed alone.[^1]

Their texture comes largely from beta-glucans, water-binding carbohydrates concentrated near the outer endosperm. Those gums are why cooked oats feel smooth and thick instead of fluffy like rice, and they are also part of what makes oats useful in porridges, dairy-like bases, and grain ferments that need body without much added starch.[^2]

Oats also behave differently in storage and processing than most other cereals. McGee notes that they carry relatively high fat levels along with an active fat-splitting enzyme, so the groats are usually given a low-temperature heat treatment first. That step keeps them from going rancid quickly, develops much of the grain's characteristic toasted aroma, and helps the groat hold together during cooking.[^2]

Most food oats are still the same whole groat underneath. Steel-cut oats are simply chopped groats; rolled oats are steamed and flattened to rehydrate quickly; quick oats are rolled thinner still.[^2] That shared origin matters here because the notebook keeps moving back and forth between intact grains, malted oats, oat milk, and enzyme-driven ferments. The ingredient changes texture faster than it changes identity.
