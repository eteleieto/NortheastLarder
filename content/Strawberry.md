*Fragaria × ananassa*

Strawberries are fragrant, sweet-tart aggregate fruits with a short, delicate post-harvest life. Fully ripe fruit is aromatic and soft, two traits that also make it quick to bruise and decay.[^1]


The red, fleshy part is enlarged receptacle tissue rather than a botanical berry. The dots on its surface are achenes—small dry fruits, each formed from a separately pollinated ovule. Incomplete pollination is one reason a strawberry can develop misshapen areas.[^4]


Maine Extension includes both traditional June-bearing cultivars and day-neutral strawberries among varieties suited to the state’s gardens.[^2] New Hampshire trials show why day-neutral plants are useful in the region: unlike June-bearers, they can flower across a broad temperature window and begin producing in the planting year, often carrying fruit from late June into November.[^7] That extended crop is a different ingredient rhythm from the short, concentrated flush of a June-bearing bed.

The ripe fruit's structure depends on water pressure inside loosely joined cells. Drying lowers that pressure, while freezing punctures cell walls; either route collapses the fresh texture.[^5] Strawberries do not improve after picking and are naturally low in pectin, so preserves often need either added pectin or a pectin-rich companion fruit.[^6] Their aroma also changes with maturity, which is why postharvest researchers use volatile profiles as one way to distinguish ripe from underripe fruit rather than relying on color alone.[^8]
