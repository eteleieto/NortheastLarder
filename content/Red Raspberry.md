*Rubus idaeus* and hybrids

Red raspberry is the familiar hollow-centered bramble fruit of northern gardens and hedgerows, and in the Northeast it sits close to the line between cultivated berry and wild foraged summer fruit.[^1] Each berry is an aggregate of many small drupelets attached to a conical receptacle, so the empty center in a picked raspberry is not damage but the ordinary result of the fruit letting go of that core.[^1]

[[Blackberry|Blackberries]] and raspberries are both *Rubus*, but they part company at harvest. A blackberry comes away with its dry plug still attached, while the raspberry leaves that plug behind on the plant, which helps explain the raspberry's lighter, hollower shape.[^2] North American red raspberry was widely used by settlers and eventually domesticated alongside European material, and commercial breeding now moves easily between summer-bearing canes that fruit in their second year and fall-bearing canes that crop on first-year growth.[^1]

In the kitchen, raspberries punch above their size because they carry a concentrated aroma built around the compound called raspberry ketone, supported by violet-like ionones.[^3] They are also rich enough in pectin for jams and preserves, but the fresh fruit is notoriously short-lived: thin skins and an unusually active metabolism make ripe raspberries among the most fragile and perishable fruits after harvest.[^1][^3]
