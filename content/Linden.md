*Tilia americana* and related *Tilia* spp.

Linden flowers are small, cream-colored, and distinctly honey-scented. The flowers and their pale leaflike bracts are the part usually gathered for infusions, with a sweet flavor that suggests honey even without added sugar.[^1]


American linden, or basswood (*Tilia americana*), is native in every New England state. The European name “lime tree” has nothing to do with citrus limes; it is another old name for trees in *Tilia*.[^2]


The flower cluster hangs from the middle of a narrow papery bract. Later, that bract acts a little like a wing as the small fruits fall from the tree. In American linden, the flowers' nectar attracts bees and contributes to the tree's value as a Northeast pollinator plant.[^3]


The honeylike aroma is already present in the flower. An infusion can suggest honey even when it contains no sweetener at all, which makes linden a useful example of how aroma can create perceived sweetness. American linden is a large native tree of New England forests and floodplains, so its flowers arrive from a very different scale of plant than the small herbs usually gathered for tea.[^2][^4]
