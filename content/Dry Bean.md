*Phaseolus vulgaris*

Dry beans are mature, dried seeds valued for their long storage life and rich, creamy texture. Maine-grown types include Yellow Eye, Jacob’s Cattle, Soldier, Navy, Black Turtle, and Vermont Cranberry beans.[^1]


Dry beans, peas, and lentils are the mature seed forms of legumes and are collectively called pulses.[^2] In the Northeast, “dry bean” can mean both a crop category and a local food tradition: Maine seed lists include named regional types such as Yellow Eye and Soldier alongside widely grown black and navy beans.[^1]

Storage history can be visible in the pot. Beans kept for months in warm, humid conditions may become “hard-to-cook” and resist softening, while beans formed during hot, dry growing conditions can develop unusually water-resistant seed coats.[^3] Cooking quality depends on the architecture of the seed as well as its age: the seed coat controls water entry, while the cotyledon cell walls must separate and soften. USDA research links thicker seed coats and more substantial cell-wall material with longer cooking times.[^4]

That variation is why two beans that look similar can behave differently in the kitchen. Color and pattern are not only visual markers; they often travel with differences in seed size, coat, starch, and texture. A roughly 1% salt soak can speed softening by displacing magnesium from cell-wall pectins; baking soda acts more strongly but can leave a slippery texture and soapy taste.[^3]
