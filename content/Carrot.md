*Daucus carota* subsp. *sativus*

Carrots are storage taproots selected into a wide range of shapes and colors, and they fit the Northeast well because they handle cool weather, sit safely in the ground for a long season, and keep well after harvest.[^1][^2] Maine Extension recommends sowing them in spring and again later in the season, which says a lot about how comfortably they sit inside a Northeastern garden rhythm.[^2]

The familiar orange carrot is relatively recent. Older cultivated carrots were purple or yellow, and the dense orange forms that dominate now were selected in the Netherlands in the seventeenth century.[^3][^4] That history still matters in the kitchen because the pigments behave differently: orange carrots carry oil-soluble carotenoids that stay put in wet cooking, while purple carrots rely on anthocyanins that bleed readily into soups, pickles, and stews.[^3][^5]

Carrot flavor is stronger and stranger than its sweet reputation suggests. McGee describes the aroma as a terpene mix that can read as pine, wood, citrus, oil, or even turpentine, with cooking adding a violet-like note as carotenoid pigments break down.[^4] The sweeter outer storage layers carry more flavor than the core, and peeling removes much of the surface bitterness along with phenolics that brown easily.[^4] That is a useful distinction here because the notebook keeps treating carrots not just as roots but as a source of savory ferments, roasted sweetness, and fragrant trim.

The plant is biennial, so if a carrot is left in the ground or replanted after storage, it shifts from root-making into flower and seed production.[^3] That matters for food use because the first-year root is the main prize, but it also hints at why carrot flavor can bridge root, leaf, and flower so naturally in the kitchen.[^3][^4]
