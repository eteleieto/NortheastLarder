*Lactuca sativa* var. *angustana*

Celtuce is a stem lettuce: a lettuce grown mainly for its thick crisp stalk rather than for a head of leaves.[^1] McGee describes it as especially popular in Asia, where the stalk is stripped, peeled, sliced, and cooked, while the younger leaves remain usable but secondary.[^1] Cornell’s vegetable-variety notes describe the stalk as thick and tender with a celery-like flavor, which is close enough to explain both the name and the confusion it causes. It is still lettuce, but it is lettuce that eats like a vegetable stem.[^2]

That stalk is the important food part. Fresh celtuce has the watery crunch and faint bitterness you would expect from a lettuce relative, but the peeled stem has more density than salad greens and stays recognizable after cooking or fermentation.[^1][^2] Like other lettuces, it bleeds white latex when cut; McGee points out that even the name *Lactuca* refers to that milky sap.[^1] The leaves can still be eaten, but the plant’s real value is that unusual middle ground between leaf and stalk.

In a Northeastern kitchen, celtuce makes sense less as a canonical regional crop than as a cool-season vegetable that can slot into the same part of the year as lettuce. Cornell’s gardening program lists it with a roughly ninety-day maturity window, which helps explain why it works as a deliberate garden vegetable rather than as an exotic one-off curiosity.[^2] That combination of crisp stem, lettuce bitterness, and enough structure to ferment or juice is exactly why it keeps turning up in experiments here.

[^1]: Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking: The Science and Lore of the Kitchen*, rev. ed. (Scribner, 2004), PDF page 333.
[^2]: Cornell University, “Celtuce Lettuce,” *Vegetable Varieties for Gardeners*, accessed August 2, 2026, https://vegvariety.cce.cornell.edu/main/detail.php?variety_id=4752.
