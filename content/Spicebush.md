*Lindera benzoin*

Spicebush is a native shrub at the northern edge of its range in Maine. It has aromatic leaves, twigs, bark, and red fall berries; the berries can be eaten fresh or dried and ground as a local spice.[^1]


Spicebush is dioecious: male and female flowers occur on separate plants, so both are needed for berry production.[^1]


The red berries have an allspice-like character, but the shrub offers more than fruit. Young leaves can be eaten, and the twigs and bark are fragrant enough to steep as a tea. A small amount of camphor in the leaves helps explain the plant's warm, resinous aroma, which is present in the leaves, flowers, and bark as well as the berries.[^1]

The fruit is technically a drupe with one seed, not a berry in the botanical sense. Its red color and fall ripening make it conspicuous just when many other fresh aromatics are disappearing, and the dried fruit can stand in for allspice because the same warm, sweet-peppery impression survives dehydration and grinding.[^2]


In Maine it is considered uncommon and favors protected, evenly moist sites, often in an understory or near a brook. The plant is dioecious, with male and female flowers on separate shrubs, so a berry-producing patch needs both sexes. Its presence is therefore more local and particular than that of common garden herbs; harvesting is most meaningful where the shrub is abundant rather than from a lone wild plant.[^1]

Across the broader Northeast, spicebush is a native understory shrub rather than a field crop. It tolerates shade and occasional wet ground, spreads slowly by root suckers, and flowers before its leaves emerge in early spring. A planted or naturally occurring patch will only fruit when male and female plants grow near one another, so the bright red autumn drupes are a useful sign of a functioning little population rather than an inevitable feature of every shrub.[^2]

The fruit’s nearly half-fat composition helps explain why its aroma feels rounder and more substantial than a dried leaf spice. The red drupe contains a single seed, while the crushed leaves, twigs, flowers, and fruit all release the shrub’s sweet, peppery scent. In food terms, spicebush is interesting precisely because several plant parts carry the same aromatic identity, but at different strengths and harvest times.[^2][^3]
