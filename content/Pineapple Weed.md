*Matricaria discoidea*

Pineapple weed is a low, rayless chamomile relative that shows up in compacted paths, yards, and other disturbed ground across New England.[^1] The rounded yellow-green flower heads look almost unfinished if you expect daisy petals, but crushing them releases the sweet tropical scent that gives the plant its common name.[^1][^2]

Both the flowers and leaves are edible, though the flower heads are the usual food part because they make the cleanest infusion.[^2] The flavor lands somewhere between chamomile and canned pineapple, with enough bitterness underneath to keep it from reading as candy.

The apparent “flower” is actually a compact head made from many tiny tubular flowers, not a single blossom. Pineapple weed lacks the white ray flowers of common chamomile, leaving a rounded yellow-green button that releases its strongest aroma when crushed.[^2][^3] The scent comes from volatile oils concentrated in the flower heads and leaves, which is why a handful of fresh plants can perfume a cup before any drying or heating has happened.

It is an especially useful foraged tea plant because it asks for almost no processing beyond picking and drying, and because its aroma is recognizable long before the plant is large. The plant favors compacted, disturbed ground, so its abundance is tied to paths, yards, and field edges rather than cultivated beds. That habitat also makes clean gathering important: a plant can be common without every patch being a suitable food source.[^1][^2]

Pineapple weed is an annual, so its presence can shift sharply from one year to the next with soil disturbance and rainfall. In food terms it is less a substantial vegetable than a fragrant herb whose flower heads carry the clearest character. The combination of tropical aroma, chamomile-like bitterness, and small edible flower heads is what makes it more interesting in the kitchen than its weedy habit first suggests.
