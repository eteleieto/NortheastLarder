Orange peel is two ingredients held together. The colored outer epidermis contains glands of aromatic oil, while the white, spongy albedo beneath is rich in pectin and protective bitter phenolics.[^1]

The oil glands are concentrated in the zest, where a light scrape releases a bright aroma without much of the white layer. The albedo contributes body and bitterness instead. Citrus peel is also a useful source of pectin, the plant cell-wall material that helps fruit gels form, so the rind can change a preserve's texture as well as its flavor.[^1] The fruit is a hesperidium: a specialized berry with an outer rind, a segmented juicy interior, and oil-bearing peel rather than the thin skin of an apple or a grape.[^1]

The bitter compounds are water-soluble but the fragrant oils are not. Repeated cold soaking or brief changes of hot water can therefore leach bitterness while retaining much of the oil; gentle cooking then softens the pectin-rich albedo before sugar is introduced for candying.[^1] That same separation helps explain why zest, whole peel, and blanched rind behave so differently in sugar, salt cures, vinegar, [[Cheong|cheong]], kosho, and shio koji. University of Maine's marmalade guidance makes the practical distinction explicit: preserving the colored rind while trimming away excess pith gives a brighter, less harsh result, while the remaining peel still supplies the pectin that helps the preserve set.[^2]

Oranges and other citrus are subtropical trees rather than Northeast field crops, so orange peel enters a regional pantry as a preserved or traded ingredient rather than a locally grown one. That does not make it incidental. In a Northeast kitchen, peel is often the part of an imported citrus fruit that gives a locally made preserve, syrup, or ferment its most recognizable top note, even when the fruit itself is not a regional crop. The colored flavedo carries the volatile perfume; the pale albedo carries much of the structural work.
