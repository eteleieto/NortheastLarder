*Cucurbita moschata* ‘Honeynut’

Honeynut is a small butternut-type squash developed through Cornell’s vegetable breeding program in New York. It is a modern cultivar, not an old miniature squash rediscovered under a new name.[^1]


Its ancestry goes back to a 1980s Cornell cross between buttercup and butternut squash. Plant breeder Michael Mazourek later refined the line with feedback from Northeast growers and chef Dan Barber, putting flavor and a compact size near the center of the breeding goal.[^2]


Honeynut begins dark green and turns caramel-orange as it ripens. Cornell selected that visible color shift as an outside signal of changes happening inside the fruit; the squash continues developing its best flavor for a time after harvest.[^1] That is useful in the Northeast, where winter squash is harvested before deep cold and then held through the months when field-grown fresh produce is scarce. Cornell’s catalog describes the fruit as reaching peak flavor one to two months after harvest.[^1]

The small size is part of the cultivar’s food identity, not just a novelty. Breeders evaluated it for flavor and texture as well as yield, and dry roasting exposed more of its natural sweetness than the watery steaming used in older trial protocols.[^2] Its orange flesh reflects carotenoid pigments, which deepen as the fruit ripens; the dense flesh and compact seed cavity make the whole squash feel more concentrated than a large butternut, even though both belong to the same broad winter-squash family.


Honeynut is a *C. moschata* squash, while [[Buttercup Squash|buttercup]] is *C. maxima*. The cultivar’s history nevertheless joins the two through breeding.
